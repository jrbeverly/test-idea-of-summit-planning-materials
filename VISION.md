# Vision Document: Summit Guide as an Instruction Booklet

## Core Idea

We want to create a proof-of-concept summit guide that lives on the web, but does not feel like a conventional event website.

The goal is to explore what a summit planning and attendee guide could become if it were treated less like a logistics page and more like a designed instruction booklet: something closer to a travel guide, field manual, game booklet, or small companion guide for entering a temporary shared world.

This should feel like a guidebook first and a website second.

The web is the medium. The summit booklet is the experience.

## Creative Direction

The inspiration is the feeling of classic game manuals and instruction booklets: visual, compact, helpful, charming, and intentionally designed.

A useful reference point is the instruction booklet aesthetic seen in _Tunic_: not to copy it literally, but to draw from the feeling that the booklet itself is part of the journey. It should feel tactile, illustrated, layered, and worth opening before the event begins.

The attendee should not feel like they are browsing a standard event microsite. They should feel like they have received a carefully made guide for arriving at a place, understanding the flow, and knowing how to move through the experience.

## What This Is Not

This should not become a generic event landing page.

It should not feel like a standard conference website, logistics portal, Notion page, PDF schedule, HR announcement, or corporate travel memo.

It should not rely on generic cards, generic countdowns, generic speaker tiles, generic agenda blocks, or generic corporate web patterns unless they have been deliberately transformed into the language of a guidebook or instruction booklet.

It should not feel like event details were dropped into a template.

The form should support the feeling of arrival, orientation, and anticipation.

## What It Should Feel Like

The experience should feel authored.

It should have the sensibility of a small object made for attendees: a guide, booklet, map, field note, travel companion, or set of illustrated pages that helps someone prepare for and move through the summit.

It may feel tactile, annotated, cartographic, playful, warm, slightly mysterious, or gently adventurous. It may use maps, routes, page numbers, diagrams, icons, stamps, arrival notes, packing hints, schedule markings, floor-plan sketches, neighbourhood cues, or small “traveller’s notes.”

These are not mandatory components. They are signals of the desired creative space.

The important thing is that the final demo should clearly reject the default visual language of modern corporate event websites.

## Relationship to the Summit

This is still a summit guide.

It should remain useful, accurate, and grounded in real attendee needs. The creative format should make the summit easier to understand, not harder.

The guide should help people answer practical questions:

Where am I going?
When should I arrive?
What happens each day?
Where do I go once I’m there?
What should I bring?
What should I expect?
Who will be there?
What is the rhythm of the event?
Where can I get help?

The best version of this concept balances logistics with atmosphere.

It should make the summit feel considered before anyone arrives.

## Experience Focus

The guide should be built around the attendee’s journey.

Rather than simply presenting information as separate pages, the concept should help someone imagine the flow of the summit: preparing, travelling, arriving, checking in, finding their way, joining sessions, taking breaks, gathering socially, and leaving with a sense of what happened.

The structure may follow the natural arc of the experience:

Before you arrive.
Getting there.
Entering the office.
Finding your bearings.
Moving through the agenda.
Knowing the key places.
Understanding the rituals and expectations.
Making the most of the time together.
Departing and following up.

This sequence is not fixed. It is a prompt for thinking about the summit as an experience rather than a list of logistics.

## Possible Content Areas

The proof of concept may include summit information such as:

- arrival instructions
- office address and entry details
- travel notes
- building access
- check-in flow
- maps and floor plans
- agenda overview
- day-by-day schedule
- session locations
- meal and break details
- nearby landmarks
- Wi-Fi and workspace information
- what to bring
- dress code or tone expectations
- accessibility notes
- emergency or support contacts
- social events
- post-summit next steps

These should be treated as possible ingredients, not a required checklist.

The implementation team should decide which content best proves the concept.

## Design Freedom

The implementation team should have room to interpret the idea.

They do not need to copy any specific game, manual, layout, map style, or illustration system. They should instead capture the broader qualities of an instruction booklet: orientation, sequencing, visual explanation, compact guidance, discovery, and a sense of place.

The guide should feel specific to the summit and the office hosting it. It should not feel like a generic event skin.

The team should feel free to invent a visual system that makes the summit feel real, memorable, and easy to navigate.

## Non-Negotiables

The demo must feel like a guidebook, manual, or instruction booklet rather than a normal event website.

It must be visually distinctive.

It must avoid generic corporate event design.

It must not feel like a standard Tailwind template with summit content added afterward.

It must make the attendee journey feel designed.

It must be useful enough that someone could imagine relying on it before and during the summit.

## Desired Outcome

The proof of concept should help people understand the possibility:

A summit guide does not have to be a plain agenda page, PDF attachment, or logistics memo. It can be a designed companion for the experience. It can help people arrive with confidence, understand the flow, and feel that the event has a shape and identity before it begins.

The goal is not to finalize the full summit system. The goal is to prove that this direction has power.

A successful demo should make someone think:

“This feels like something I would want to open before I travel.”

And just as importantly:

“This makes the summit feel intentional before I even arrive.”
