Before You Leave

What to pack, what to know, and what to expect before you head out the door.

You are about to travel to a summit. This section covers what to pack, what to know before you leave, and what the first moments will feel like when you arrive.

Read it through once, then come back when you are filling your bag. The packing list on the next spread has you covered.

1
§1

What to Pack

An illustrated packing list grouped by category.

You do not need much. The office has most of what you will want — coffee, snacks, Wi‑Fi, quiet corners — and the summit provides the rest. Pack light and bring layers.

Essentials

These are the things you will be unhappy without.

Your ID. If you are travelling from outside the city, keep it somewhere easy to reach. You will not need it at check-in, but you will need it to travel.

A notebook and pen. Sessions are conversational, not lecture-style. You will want to write things down — a thought, a question, a name. A notebook does not buzz, glow, or run out of battery.

Comfortable shoes. You will be on your feet more than you think — between sessions, during breaks, walking to lunch, exploring the neighbourhood. The office is not vast, but the day adds up.

A water bottle. There is a kitchen with glasses, but having your own bottle means you will actually drink water instead of just coffee. Keep it filled. Stay hydrated. It helps.

Your phone, charged. You will want it for photos, notes, and the occasional check-in with the outside world. Bring a charging cable. Outlets are plentiful but never quite where you left them.

Before you leave home: ID, tickets, wallet, phone, charging cable, notebook, pen. Everything else can be solved. — The five-second pockets check

Comfort

These are the things that make the difference between getting through the day and enjoying it.

A jacket or warm layer. The office runs cold — old building, tall ceilings, heating that tries its best. A sweater, fleece, or light jacket you can slip on and off is worth its weight in comfort. Trust us on this.

A small bag or day pack. Something light you can carry between rooms. The coat room has space for larger bags, but you will want your notebook, water bottle, and phone with you through the day.

Headphones. The quiet lounge is available all day, but sometimes you need your own sound. A pair of headphones — for music, for focus, for a moment alone in a room full of people — is a small gift to your future self.

An extra layer for the evening. Evenings can be cool, especially if you walk to dinner or linger outside between sessions. The welcome gathering on day one runs late. Bring one more layer than you think you need.

Tech

The office has Wi‑Fi, power strips, and a few spare chargers. Bring your own and you will never have to hunt for one.

Laptop or tablet (optional). Most sessions do not require a screen. Some attendees prefer to take notes by hand. Bring a laptop if you want to work on something between sessions, catch up on messages, or take digital notes. The Wi‑Fi network name and access details are in section 4.

Charging cable and a small power bank. The outlets are well-placed but finite. A power bank means you can charge from anywhere — the quiet lounge, the kitchen, the patch of floor by the window that gets the best light.

A dongle or adapter if you present. If you are facilitating a session and plan to share your screen, bring whatever adapter connects your laptop to a standard display. The main hall and workshop room both have screens available.

WEATHER NOTE The summit is in early autumn. Daytime temperatures are mild — light jacket weather. Mornings and evenings are cooler. Rain is possible but not likely. Check the forecast the night before you travel.

Optional

Bring these if they make you happy. Leave them if they do not.

A book. There is down time. The quiet lounge has a small shelf of things left by previous visitors. Add to it.

Snacks to share. The kitchen will be stocked, but a bag of something from your home city — chocolate, biscuits, the thing your local bakery does well — is a quiet way to start a conversation.

A camera. Your phone camera is fine. If you have a real camera and enjoy using it, bring it. Some of the best photos from past gatherings were taken by attendees who just like taking pictures.

Something that reminds you of home. A postcard, a small object, a sticker on your notebook. These things come up in conversation. They are not required. They are just nice.

What Not to Bring

A suit. You will be the only one wearing one, and you will feel overdressed. The tone is casual-but-considered. Wear what makes you feel like yourself, slightly elevated.

A printed agenda. The schedule is here, in the guide, and it will not change. Save the paper.

An expectation to perform. You are not being evaluated. You are not being scored. You are here to participate, not to prove something. Bring your curiosity. Leave the rest.

The best thing you can bring is an open question — something you are genuinely curious about.

The office runs cold — trust us on the jacket.

What to Expect on Arrival Day

A warm, anticipatory preview of the first moments at the summit.

You will know you are in the right place when you see the green door. It is the only green door on Summit Street — an old brick building, east side of the block, halfway down. You cannot miss it.

Push it open. It is unlocked from 8:15 am. The entry hall is small, warm-lit, with a bench and hooks along the right wall. The welcome desk is ahead on your left, past a glass inner door. Someone will be there to greet you by name.

The First Few Minutes

Check-in takes about a minute. You will get your badge and a small printed programme card. The welcome host will point out where to drop your coat, where the coffee is, and where the restrooms are — the things you actually want to know in the first thirty seconds after arriving.

After that, the time is yours. Coffee is in the kitchen, to the left of the main hall. The quiet lounge is open if you need a moment. The morning circle begins at 9:15 in the main hall — until then, settle in at your own pace, find a comfortable spot, and say hello to whoever is already there.

The Rhythm of the First Day

The first day is paced for arrival. You are not expected to be at full energy by 9 am. The morning eases in — a welcome circle, a light activity to get people talking, a walkthrough of how the summit works. The afternoon gives you choices: parallel sessions in the main hall and workshop room, a long break, a closing circle to reflect. The evening is a welcome gathering at the office — drinks, food, music, no agenda.

If you arrive feeling tired from travel, that is expected. The day is designed so you can participate fully without having to perform.

The full day one schedule has the details. The entry sequence page walks through the door-to-desk path step by step. And section 7, How We Gather, covers what to expect from sessions, breaks, and the unwritten culture of the summit.

What It Will Feel Like

It will feel like a group of people who are genuinely glad to be in the same room. It will feel warm but not forced. Structured but not scripted. There will be coffee, and natural light, and a few moments of quiet between the conversations.

Some people will know each other. Some people will know no one. By the end of the first morning, that distinction will have mostly dissolved.

The best version of a summit does not feel like an event being run. It feels like a place being made, together, by the people in it.

You are part of that. Arrive when you can. Bring what you need. Everything else is already here, waiting for you.

Look for the green door on the east side.
